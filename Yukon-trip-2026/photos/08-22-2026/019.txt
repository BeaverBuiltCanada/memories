The 60th Parallel — Where the North Begins  
The boundaries within Canada shifted many times before the country took its present form. In 1862, the northern border of the colony of British Columbia was set at 62°N, chosen because it followed the Stikine River basin where gold had recently been discovered. In 1863, the boundary was moved south to 60°N, and when British Columbia became a province in 1871, 60°N remained its northern limit.

The decision to shift the border south may have been influenced by the fact that 60°N aligns closely with the latitude where Yukon’s western boundary begins along the 141st meridian. By then, the Stikine gold deposits were nearly exhausted.

Long before provincial and territorial borders were drawn, the Kaska Dena traveled, traded, hunted, and trapped throughout a traditional territory that spans what is now southeastern Yukon, northwestern British Columbia, and southern Northwest Territories.

Creation of Yukon  
Until 1898, Yukon was known as the District of Yukon and formed part of the Northwest Territories. The gold rush that followed George Dawson’s 1887 geological survey brought thousands of miners into the region, but the Northwest Territories’ borders prevented access to key gold fields. This led to the creation of Dawson City and ultimately the formation of Yukon Territory.

Yukon Territory was officially created on June 13, 1898, with the 60th parallel established as its southern boundary. The land between British Columbia and Yukon was surveyed and marked between 1899–1901 and again in 1907–08. At the time, the federal government did not consider it necessary to survey the entire 60th parallel, as the region was remote from major river systems and far from the Alaska border.
