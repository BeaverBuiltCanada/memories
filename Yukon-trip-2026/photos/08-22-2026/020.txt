The Dawson Expedition  
In 1887, the Geological and Natural History Survey of Canada launched an expedition to explore and document the Yukon District. Placer gold activity was increasing, yet the federal government had almost no reliable information about this remote region. George Mercer Dawson led the expedition, bringing expertise in geology, natural history, and precise mechanical surveying.

Dawson divided the work into three parties: William Ogilvie led one, Richard McConnell another, while Dawson himself followed a historic packhorse route first traveled nearly forty years earlier by Robert Campbell of the Hudson’s Bay Company. Their journey carried them from the Pacific coast up the Stikine, Liard, and Frances rivers to Frances Lake. The expedition demanded travel by packhorse, handmade boats, and long stretches on foot. Dawson endured significant physical challenges, including spinal tuberculosis that left him with a pronounced curvature and limited height.

Despite the hardships, the expedition produced extensive geological, geographical, and ethnological records. These findings became critically important a decade later when gold was discovered in the Klondike.

Place Names  
Dawson documented many Kaska place names around Frances Lake, likely provided by the Kaska guides who accompanied him north from Lower Post. Many of these names remain recognized by Kaska speakers today.

Survey Routes  
William Ogilvie crossed the Chilkoot Pass and traveled down the Yukon River to the 141st meridian. Richard McConnell surveyed the Stikine and Lower Liard rivers. Ogilvie later played a key role during the Klondike gold rush as the first surveyor of the goldfields. Dawson City was named in honor of George Mercer Dawson and served as Yukon’s seat of government for more than fifty years.

Dawson City  
In recognition of Dawson’s contributions to mapping and surveying the territory, the new capital of the Klondike was named after him. Dawson City remained the administrative center of Yukon for over half a century.
