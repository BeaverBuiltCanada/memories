Travel & Transportation  
Traditionally, the Kaska traveled mainly over land rather than by river, carrying their belongings on their backs or using pack dogs. Rivers and creeks served as connections to neighboring groups, but the many rapids and portages made water travel difficult.

Until the mid‑20th century, outsiders reached this region by long, demanding routes from Wrangell, Alaska, following the Stikine, Dease, and Liard rivers, or by traveling east along the Finlay River and down the Mackenzie River. These journeys often took weeks. Kaska people supplemented their subsistence lifestyle by working on riverboats, cutting wood, or providing fur.

The construction of the Alaska Highway in 1942 shifted transportation from river routes to land routes. Many Kaska worked as guides for the U.S. Army or as laborers during construction. The highway brought supplies, new roads, increased air travel, and new opportunities. It also disrupted traditional travel and trade routes while creating new ones. Once the highway was completed, freight that once traveled by river could move by road, allowing many Kaska families to relocate from places like Frances Lake and Polly Banks to communities along the highway such as Two Mile, Upper Liard, and Watson Lake.

<h5>Liard Tom</h5>
Liard Tom, a Kaska Dena, traveled widely by dog team to hunt and trap. His deep knowledge of the land made him an essential guide for U.S. Army survey crews during the building of the Alaska Highway. He also worked for the Hudson’s Bay Company constructing stores and trading posts. In recognition of his contributions, Liard Tom was inducted into the Yukon Transportation Hall of Fame in 2014.
