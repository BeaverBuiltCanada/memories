<h5>Swing across, inch forward, then back again</h5>

Dredges traveled slowly upstream. During one eight-month season, a dredge moved just 0.8 km (0.5 mile), working 24 hours a day, 7 days per week.

To dig, the spud was lowered; winches and cables then swung it slowly in an arc, catching every last bit of gold-bearing gravel in the valley bottom.

To move forward, the spud was lifted and the dredge was winched forward using cables attached to “deadmen,” logs buried in the hillside.

The water gushed out the flume! Worked those dig piles all “tootsie called ‘flumey.’” Percy Cundill, Dredge Captain

During the season, dredges took away all the stream, all the trees, all the moss. Percy Cundill, Dredge Captain

Dredge No. 4 worked its fourth pit on the Klondike River, and then it finished its career in 1959. It was abandoned in its last tailings pile, where it remains today. Percy Cundill, Dredge Captain
