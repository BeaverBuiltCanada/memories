We visited the Jack London museum and met a very nice little old lady who was full of stories. She was very passionate about Jack.
She told us a story, kinda goes like this.
Jack London built a small cabin on Henderson Creek during the Klondike Gold Rush in 1897–98. Decades later, trappers rediscovered the abandoned structure and noticed a faint signature carved into one of the logs — Jack London, Jan. 27, 1898. That inscription became the crucial clue.

Yukon researcher Dick North spent years tracking down records and witnesses to prove the cabin was authentic. A handwriting expert from the Oakland Police Department later confirmed the signature matched London’s.

Once verified, both Canada and the United States wanted the cabin. Rather than fight over it, they reached a unique agreement:
the cabin was dismantled and the logs were split between the two countries.

Dawson City rebuilt its version using the original bottom logs, while Oakland, California used the other half to create a matching replica. Today, both cities claim a piece of Jack London’s northern history — literally.
<h5>Jack London — Northern Years</h5>
Jack London came north during the Klondike Gold Rush, long before he became a famous author. He wasn’t a prospector for long — sickness and hardship pushed him out — but the people he met and the landscapes he crossed stayed with him. Those northern experiences shaped the stories that later made him famous, especially The Call of the Wild, inspired by a real dog named Buck and the harsh world of the Yukon. His time here was short, but it changed the direction of his writing forever.
